<Section icon={Users} title="Co-Op Roles and Communication Setup">

A reliable team should assign responsibilities before entering a difficult section. The source gameplay repeatedly demonstrates how confusion develops when both players try to control the same moment. A simple role system reduces unnecessary corrections and keeps instructions short.

The strongest setup uses one primary mover and one spotter. The primary mover handles the current direction or push. The spotter watches for spikes, poison hazards, walls, and route changes. These roles can change at checkpoints, but switching during an obstacle is risky.

<CardGrid cols={3}>
  <Card title="Primary Mover">
    - Controls the current direction
    - Uses short, measured inputs
    - Stops when the spotter calls a hazard
  </Card>
  <Card title="Hazard Spotter">
    - Watches the next obstacle
    - Calls left, right, stop, or slow
    - Avoids giving several commands at once
  </Card>
  <Card title="Checkpoint Caller">
    - Confirms when progress is saved
    - Tracks the safest restart point
    - Helps the team reset after a failed attempt
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

| Callout | Meaning | Best Use |
|---|---|---|
| “Stop” | Release movement immediately | Approaching spikes or a bad angle |
| “Slow” | Use a short input | Narrow ledges and tight turns |
| “Left” or “Right” | Commit to one direction | Aligning with a safe route |
| “Reset” | Stop forcing the current attempt | Returning to a known checkpoint |
| “Go” | Begin the planned movement | After the route is confirmed |

<Callout type="warning" title="Avoid Mixed Commands">
Do not call “left,” “right,” and “stop” in rapid succession unless the situation is changing. Contradictory instructions can be more dangerous than the obstacle itself.

</Callout>

Use a consistent vocabulary throughout the run. Long explanations are difficult to follow during a hazard sequence. Short commands create a shared rhythm:

- **Stop** before changing direction.
- **Slow** near spikes or poison.
- **Push** only after the landing area is clear.
- **Hold** when a steady input is needed.
- **Reset** when the current position is worse than the checkpoint.

If both players want to lead, alternate leadership after each checkpoint. This keeps the session cooperative without forcing a mid-obstacle argument over control.

</Section>

<Section icon={Shield} title="Obstacle Handling and Recovery Tips">

Obstacle sections become easier when the team separates movement from observation. One player should think about the current position, while the other looks ahead. This division prevents both players from reacting to the same visual threat at once.

Spikes require early braking. If the team waits until the character is directly beside a spike, there may not be enough space to correct the angle. Poison hazards demand the same caution, especially when a push or bounce can carry the character farther than expected.

| Hazard Type | Safe Response | Risky Response |
|---|---|---|
| Spikes | Stop early and align before passing | Hold movement until the last moment |
| Poison area | Use small inputs and check spacing | Push quickly without checking distance |
| Wall or tree | Reposition before forcing movement | Repeatedly push into the obstruction |
| Bounce surface | Confirm the landing direction | Launch without checking the next hazard |
| Wrong-way route | Stop and compare the path | Continue because progress seems possible |

<Callout type="warning" title="Recovery Before Speed">
A recovery attempt should prioritize a stable position, not immediate forward progress. Moving backward briefly may create a better angle for the next push.

</Callout>

A useful recovery sequence is:

1. Release all movement.
2. Identify the nearest safe surface.
3. Decide whether to move forward, backward, or reset.
4. Give one player control.
5. Make one small correction.
6. Pause and reassess.

This method is especially helpful when the characters become stuck near a wall or obstacle. Continuous inputs can make the position worse, while a brief release often reveals which direction is actually available.

The team should also treat mistakes as route information. If a specific angle causes a launch into a spike, mark that angle as unsafe. If a wall bounce creates useful distance, repeat it only after confirming the landing area. The objective is controlled experimentation rather than random repetition.

</Section>

- **Third run:** Reduce unnecessary commands and tighten timing.
- **Later runs:** Attempt smoother transitions without sacrificing control.
