<Section icon={Target} title="How to Control Shared Movement">

Shared movement is easier when each player understands their responsibility. The player giving directions should watch the upcoming route, hazards, and available space. The player applying movement should prioritize precision and avoid adding extra inputs that were not requested.

A simple communication system keeps the team focused:

- **“Push”** means begin a small movement input.
- **“Hold”** means maintain the current direction briefly.
- **“Release”** means stop adding force or direction.
- **“Slow”** means reduce momentum before an obstacle.
- **“Reset”** means stop forcing the route and prepare for another attempt.

| Player Role | Primary Job | Best Communication |
|---|---|---|
| Caller | Read the route and identify hazards | Short directional commands |
| Controller | Apply measured movement | Confirm inputs before changing direction |
| Spotter | Watch walls, spikes, and edges | Warn before the danger zone |
| Reset Lead | Decide when a route is no longer safe | Call for a controlled restart |

<Callout type="warning" title="Avoid Conflicting Inputs">
When both players try to correct the route at the same moment, the team may move backward or veer into a hazard. Use one caller and one controller until the section is clear.
</Callout>

The most difficult moments usually happen after a mistake, when players rush to recover lost progress. Instead, pause for a moment and identify the next safe surface. A short delay is often better than another uncontrolled launch.

A useful practice routine is to separate movement into three phases:

1. **Approach:** Build enough momentum to reach the next area.
2. **Correction:** Reduce movement and line up with the safe route.
3. **Commitment:** Move through the opening only after both players agree.

This method keeps the team from making large adjustments while already surrounded by hazards.

</Section>

<Section icon={Shield} title="Hazards, Timing, and Recovery">

Hazards are designed to make uncontrolled movement costly. Spikes can punish a direct push, poisonous areas can block a careless route, and walls can redirect the team unexpectedly. The safest response is to identify the hazard before building momentum.

| Hazard Type | Risk | Safer Response |
|---|---|---|
| Spikes | Immediate loss of position or failed attempt | Stop early and approach at a shallow angle |
| Poison area | Unsafe landing or forced detour | Keep the route centered and avoid panic corrections |
| Wall edge | Sudden bounce or direction change | Use light inputs near the boundary |
| Narrow opening | Easy to overshoot | Agree on a slow approach before entering |
| Wrong-way route | Lost time and poor positioning | Stop, communicate, and retrace carefully |

<Steps>
  <Step num="1" title="Identify the Safe Surface">
    Before moving, locate the platform, opening, or route segment that keeps the team away from the nearest hazard. Do not begin with a large input while the destination is unclear.
  </Step>
  <Step num="2" title="Build Small Momentum">
    Use a controlled push toward the safe surface. Short inputs provide more opportunities to correct the route before reaching a spike, wall, or poisonous area.
  </Step>
  <Step num="3" title="Call the Correction">
    As the team approaches the obstacle, the caller should announce the required direction once. The controller should make one adjustment instead of repeatedly switching sides.
  </Step>
  <Step num="4" title="Confirm the Landing">
    After reaching a safer position, pause briefly and verify the next route. This prevents the team from immediately drifting back into danger.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="success" title="Recovery Rule">
A failed attempt is useful when it reveals one specific mistake. Remember whether the problem was speed, direction, timing, or communication, then change that factor on the next try.
</Callout>

Checkpoints change the way teams should think about failure. Once a checkpoint is reached, the goal is no longer to preserve the entire run at any cost. The team can take a more measured approach because a failed attempt may return them to a closer starting point.

| Recovery Situation | Recommended Decision | Why It Helps |
|---|---|---|
| Near a checkpoint | Slow down and secure the route | Prevents an avoidable miss |
| Stuck against a wall | Stop adding random inputs | Creates space for a planned correction |
| Facing the wrong direction | Confirm the route before moving | Avoids repeating the same mistake |
| Inside a dangerous section | Use controlled movement or reset | Protects the next attempt |
| Repeating the same failure | Change one variable | Makes practice measurable |

The key is to avoid treating every failure as a reason to move faster. Controlled recovery creates more information, and that information makes later attempts more consistent.

<Section icon={Sparkles} title="Practice Strategy for Consistent Progress">

Teams improve faster when they practice one movement skill at a time. Trying to master speed, hazard timing, route reading, and communication simultaneously can make it difficult to identify what went wrong.

Start with open sections and practice moving in a straight direction while using short commands. Once both players can stop reliably, add simple turns. After that, practice approaching hazards without rushing.

<Callout type="tip" title="Practice Tip">
If the team keeps failing in the same place, stop trying to complete the whole section. Practice only the final turn or hazard approach until the movement becomes familiar.
</Callout>

A strong team also knows when to switch roles. If the caller is missing hazards, the controller can briefly describe what is visible. If the controller is overcorrecting, the caller should reduce commands and allow more time between inputs.

The goal is not perfect speed. It is predictable movement. A slower team that communicates clearly can often advance farther than a faster team that changes direction without warning.
