<Section icon={Users} title="Cooperative Movement and Communication">

The most important skill is deciding who has control during each part of the course. Both players can contribute, but simultaneous corrections often create the exact movement neither player intended. A simple leader-and-support system makes the controls easier to manage.

The leader calls the next direction and controls the timing of the push. The support player watches for spikes, poison areas, walls, or unexpected reversals. After the character reaches a stable position, the players can switch roles if the next obstacle requires a different perspective.

This system is especially helpful when the route becomes confusing. The gameplay demonstrates how quickly teams can travel backward when they lose track of the intended path. Calling out “forward,” “left,” or “stop” before making an input gives both players a shared plan.

These roles do not need to remain fixed for an entire run. A player who has a better view of the next section should usually become the caller. If the route contains a difficult timing challenge, the player with better control over the current direction can lead that attempt.

| Communication Callout | Meaning | Best Use |
|---|---|---|
| “Forward” | Continue toward the next opening | Clear straight sections |
| “Short push” | Apply limited movement | Narrow platforms |
| “Stop” | Release the current input | Before spikes or edges |
| “Back to checkpoint” | Abandon a failing attempt | When recovery is unlikely |
| “Hold” | Maintain the current alignment | Waiting for a safe timing window |

<Callout type="success" title="Team Play Tip">
Use one clear command at a time. Repeating several directions in quick succession can make a difficult section harder, especially when the character is already moving.
</Callout>

A useful communication loop is simple: identify the obstacle, choose a direction, make one controlled input, and reassess. This method prevents players from reacting emotionally after a mistake. It also makes successful movement easier to repeat.

</Section>

<Section icon={Shield} title="Hazards, Timing, and Safer Inputs">

Hazards are the main reason Fart With Your Friends becomes difficult even when the route looks short. Spikes can appear beside or beyond a safe platform, while poison-like areas and red obstacles restrict the available path. The danger is not only touching an obstacle; it is also entering a position where the next correction becomes impossible.

The safest approach is to divide each obstacle into small movement decisions. First, identify the surface or opening where the character must land. Next, choose the direction that creates the smallest correction. Finally, release or change direction only after the character is aligned.

| Hazard Type | What Makes It Difficult | Safer Technique |
|---|---|---|
| Spikes | Small mistakes cause immediate failure | Approach slowly and center the character |
| Poison area | Limits safe landing space | Confirm the exit before entering |
| Red obstacle | Requires precise positioning | Use one player to call timing |
| Narrow edge | Momentum can carry the character away | Tap the direction instead of holding it |
| Unexpected wall | Reverses or blocks movement | Pause, then choose a new angle |

Momentum management is more important than speed. Holding a direction for too long can launch the character beyond a checkpoint or into a hazard. Short inputs provide more opportunities to correct the route, while rushed movement often creates a second problem before the first one is solved.

When a section contains two hazards on opposite sides, each player should focus on a different threat. One player can monitor the obstacle ahead, while the other watches the side that could cause a sideways drift. This division of attention is more effective than both players reacting to the same hazard.

<Callout type="warning" title="Hazard Warning">
Do not keep pushing after the character begins drifting toward a hazard. Release the input, reassess the angle, and only then make the next correction.
</Callout>

A failed attempt does not always mean the route is misunderstood. Sometimes the team has the correct direction but uses too much force or changes direction too late. Before changing the entire strategy, try the same route with slower inputs and a more deliberate stop point.

</Section>

<Section icon={Target} title="Step-by-Step Checkpoint Progression">

Checkpoints change how teams should approach the course. Once a checkpoint is reached, the goal is no longer to repeat the entire route from the beginning. The team should focus on stabilizing the current position, identifying the next hazard, and making progress in small sections.

<Steps>
  <Step num="1" title="Confirm the Current Route">
    Before moving, identify the next safe platform or opening. Agree on whether the character needs to go forward, left, right, or backward. Avoid pressing multiple directions while the route is still unclear.
  </Step>
  <Step num="2" title="Assign the Active Caller">
    Choose one player to announce the movement direction. The second player should focus on hazard awareness and timing rather than issuing competing commands.
  </Step>
  <Step num="3" title="Use a Short Input">
    Apply a controlled push instead of holding the direction continuously. Short movement gives the team time to check alignment and react before reaching a spike or edge.
  </Step>
  <Step num="4" title="Stabilize Near the Checkpoint">
    Once the character reaches a checkpoint or safe area, stop unnecessary movement. Confirm the next route only after the character is no longer drifting.
  </Step>
  <Step num="5" title="Reset When the Attempt Is Lost">
    If the character is trapped or moving toward an unavoidable hazard, stop trying to save the position through frantic inputs. Recover from the checkpoint and repeat the section with a smaller correction.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The checkpoint strategy is most effective when teams separate progress from experimentation. Use the first attempt after a checkpoint to learn the route. On the next attempt, reproduce the successful direction and refine only the part that failed.

| Progress Phase | Team Objective | Common Mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Route reading | Find the next safe opening | Moving before agreeing |
| First attempt | Test timing and angle | Holding movement too long |
| Checkpoint landing | Stop and stabilize | Continuing unnecessary inputs |
| Repeat attempt | Reproduce the successful line | Changing too many variables |
| Recovery | Return to the latest safe point | Panicking near the hazard |

<Callout type="info" title="Checkpoint Method">
A checkpoint is more than a respawn location. Use it as a practice marker: learn one section, stabilize, then apply the same movement pattern on the next attempt.
</Callout>

Progress may feel slow when the team pauses often, but pauses create consistency. The fastest-looking approach is not always the quickest way to finish a difficult obstacle course. A careful team spends less time repeating avoidable mistakes.

</Section>
