<Section icon={Users} title="Co-op Roles and Communication">

The strongest team structure uses temporary roles rather than permanent character classes. One player acts as the **caller**, describing the next direction and warning about hazards. The other player acts as the **driver**, applying the movement input and adjusting speed. These roles can switch whenever the course requires a different type of control.

The caller should not give a constant stream of instructions. Too many commands can make the driver react late or reverse direction by mistake. Short phrases such as “hold left,” “slow,” “stop,” or “right after the bounce” are easier to follow during difficult sections.

| Team role | Primary responsibility | Best communication | Common mistake |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Caller | Read the route and announce hazards | Short, early commands | Giving directions too late |
| Driver | Apply movement and control momentum | Confirming each command | Overcorrecting after contact |
| Spotter | Watch the next obstacle | Warning before the screen shifts | Focusing only on the current hazard |
| Reset leader | Decide when a failed attempt should restart | Clear checkpoint callout | Continuing a doomed movement |

In some sections, both players may need to contribute equally. A bounce or launch area can require one player to push in a direction while the other prevents the shared character from drifting into a hazard. In these moments, the best approach is to establish which input has priority before moving.

A useful rule is to separate **navigation** from **correction**. Navigation decides where the team should go. Correction handles small adjustments after the movement begins. If both players try to solve both tasks simultaneously, the shared character may drift backward, launch in the wrong direction, or return to a previous section.

<Callout type="info" title="Communication Rule">
The player giving directions should call the intended movement, while the player controlling momentum should confirm whether the input is safe to execute.
</Callout>

<Section icon={Target} title="Step-by-Step Course Strategy">

The cooperative course becomes more manageable when each attempt follows the same routine. Do not treat every failure as a reason to improvise. Instead, use the checkpoint system to isolate the problem, then repeat the section with one deliberate adjustment.

<Steps>
  <Step num="1" title="Locate the Current Checkpoint">
    Before moving, identify where the team will respawn if the attempt fails. Knowing the recovery point helps you decide whether to continue, reset, or test a risky route.
  </Step>
  <Step num="2" title="Read the Next Hazard">
    Look for spikes, walls, bounce surfaces, narrow gaps, or poisonous-looking obstacles. Decide the desired direction before either player applies strong movement.
  </Step>
  <Step num="3" title="Assign Control">
    Choose one caller and one driver for the segment. The caller gives the next direction, while the driver controls the shared character’s momentum.
  </Step>
  <Step num="4" title="Use Controlled Inputs">
    Start with a light movement and increase pressure only when the route is clear. Near hazards, small adjustments are easier to recover from than a sudden launch.
  </Step>
  <Step num="5" title="Confirm the Landing">
    After reaching a safe platform or checkpoint, stop briefly and reassign roles if necessary. Do not rush into the next obstacle while the team is still repositioning.
  </Step>
</Steps>

| Course situation | Recommended input style | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open path | Moderate movement | Builds progress without unnecessary hesitation |
| Spike corridor | Light, measured input | Reduces the chance of drifting into hazards |
| Bounce section | Directional correction | Keeps the launch angle under control |
| Confusing intersection | Stop and communicate | Prevents both players from choosing opposite routes |
| Post-checkpoint area | Brief pause | Allows the team to reset roles |

The most important habit is slowing down before danger appears. Many failed attempts begin when players enter a hazard at full momentum and only then try to correct. By that point, the available reaction window may be too short.

If the team reaches a dead end or returns to the beginning of a section, use the failure as information. Ask whether the problem came from the route, the timing, the direction, or conflicting inputs. Change one factor on the next attempt so you can identify what improved the result.

<Callout type="success" title="Progress Method">
After each failed attempt, change one variable only: speed, direction timing, role assignment, or route choice. This makes difficult sections easier to learn.
</Callout>

<Section icon={Shield} title="Hazards, Checkpoints, and Recovery">

The main danger in the cooperative mode is not simply touching an obstacle. It is losing control of the shared movement while trying to avoid one. A quick correction may solve one problem but create another, especially when hazards appear on both sides of the route.

Checkpoints change the way teams should approach risk. Once a checkpoint is secured, the goal is no longer to protect the entire run from the beginning. Instead, players can focus on learning the next segment. If a position becomes unrecoverable, resetting to the checkpoint is often more efficient than continuing to force movement.

| Hazard type | Warning sign | Safer response | Recovery advice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spikes | Sharp obstacles near the route | Reduce speed and align early | Restart from the latest checkpoint |
| Bounce surface | Launch or rebound section | Agree on direction before contact | Repeat with a smaller angle |
| Narrow passage | Limited room for correction | Use short movement inputs | Assign one driver |
| Wrong-way drift | Shared character moves backward | Stop competing inputs | Reconfirm the route |
| Obstructed view | Hazard hidden by scenery | Pause and scan the path | Let the caller lead |

A team should also distinguish between a **mistake** and a **bad state**. A mistake may be corrected with a small input. A bad state occurs when the shared character is already aimed at an unavoidable hazard or trapped against scenery. Continuing to press buttons in a bad state can waste time and create confusion. Call for a reset, then try again with a revised plan.

Players looking to improve should track repeatable behaviors rather than only counting completed sections. The goal is to build a reliable team process that works across different obstacle patterns.

A strong practice session can follow three phases. First, play slowly and focus on understanding the route. Second, repeat the same segment while reducing unnecessary pauses. Third, attempt a smoother run while keeping communication concise. This approach is more consistent than rushing for speed before the team understands the hazards.

| Practice goal | What to measure | Useful result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Better communication | Fewer conflicting commands | Cleaner movement decisions |
| Better control | Fewer large corrections | More stable positioning |
| Better recovery | Faster checkpoint resets | Less time lost after failure |
| Better consistency | More successful repeated sections | Stronger route memory |

The game’s cooperative design also makes patience part of the strategy. A team may understand the correct route and still fail because the movement starts too quickly or the direction changes too late. Treat the early attempts as route discovery, then use the checkpoint to refine timing.

<Callout type="tip" title="Team Improvement Tip">
Celebrate controlled progress, not only finishes. Reaching a checkpoint with fewer corrections is a meaningful improvement because it shows that the team is learning the mode.
