<Section icon={Target} title="Core Movement and Direction Tips">

The demo rewards controlled movement rather than frantic corrections. When the route is open, stronger propulsion can help the team progress. Near a spike or narrow passage, however, a full push may create too much momentum to recover safely.

A useful rhythm is to move, observe, and then adjust. Do not assume that the first direction is correct simply because it worked for a few seconds. Obstacles can force a new angle, and a team may accidentally begin traveling backward if no one confirms the route.

| Situation | Best Input Style | Common Mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Open path | Moderate forward push | Holding movement without checking the route |
| Near spikes | Light, short adjustments | Overcorrecting into the hazard |
| Narrow passage | Slow movement with frequent stops | Trying to pass at maximum speed |
| After bouncing off an edge | Re-center before pushing again | Continuing the original input automatically |
| Unclear route | Pause and identify the next safe lane | Guessing based on panic calls |

<Steps>
  <Step num="1" title="Choose the Direction Caller">
    Before moving, decide which player has final responsibility for route calls. The second player should watch hazards, confirm the direction, and warn about upcoming changes.
  </Step>
  <Step num="2" title="Start With a Small Push">
    Begin with a controlled movement input instead of immediately using maximum propulsion. This creates room to evaluate the route and prevents an early collision.
  </Step>
  <Step num="3" title="Read the Next Hazard">
    Identify spikes, poison areas, walls, and the next checkpoint before reaching the current obstacle. Planning one section ahead reduces last-second turns.
  </Step>
  <Step num="4" title="Release and Reposition">
    When the route becomes unstable, stop pushing and allow the movement to settle. Repositioning is usually safer than adding more force to correct a bad angle.
  </Step>
  <Step num="5" title="Confirm the Checkpoint">
    After reaching a checkpoint, pause briefly and verify the new route. Do not rush forward simply because the team has made progress.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="warning" title="Avoid Direction Conflicts">
Two players giving opposite commands can cancel progress or send the team into a hazard. Use one primary caller, and let the second player provide short warnings instead of competing instructions.
</Callout>

A strong communication pattern is concise and repeatable:

- **“Forward”** means begin or maintain the route.
- **“Left” or “right”** identifies the next correction.
- **“Slow”** means reduce propulsion before a hazard.
- **“Stop”** means pause immediately and reassess.
- **“Checkpoint”** confirms that the team can plan from a safer position.

<Section icon={Shield} title="Hazard Handling and Checkpoint Recovery">

Hazards are most dangerous when the team reaches them without a shared plan. Spikes can appear alongside the route, behind the movement path, or near a bounce point. Poison hazards and other red danger areas also require careful positioning because an aggressive correction may solve one problem while creating another.

The safest approach is to divide the route into short sections. Move only far enough to reach the next stable position, then reassess. This prevents one mistake from turning into a long uncontrolled slide.

| Hazard Type | Warning Sign | Safer Approach | Recovery Advice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spike wall | Sharp obstacle beside the route | Enter slowly and keep the movement centered | Stop pushing if the angle becomes uncertain |
| Poison area | Dangerous colored zone near the path | Use a light adjustment and avoid unnecessary bouncing | Return to the checkpoint after a failed attempt |
| Narrow edge | Limited space for correction | Make small directional inputs | Re-center before attempting the next push |
| Bounce section | Momentum changes after contact | Expect a new angle immediately | Call the next direction before landing |
| Wrong-way route | Progress begins moving backward | Stop and verify the intended path | Reset if returning is safer than forcing forward |

<Callout type="success" title="Checkpoint Discipline">
A checkpoint is more than a progress marker. Use it as a reset point for communication: identify the next hazard, assign the caller, and agree on whether the next movement should be light or strong.
</Callout>

When an attempt becomes unstable, forcing the route may waste more time than restarting from the latest checkpoint. A deliberate reset can be useful when the team is facing the wrong direction, trapped against an obstacle, or repeatedly entering the same hazard.

The reset decision should follow three questions:

- Are both players facing the intended route?
- Can the current position be corrected with a small movement?
- Would restarting from the checkpoint provide a cleaner angle?

If the answer to the first two questions is no, stop adding inputs. A controlled failure is often easier to recover from than a sequence of increasingly aggressive corrections.

</Section>

<Section icon={Swords} title="Cooperative Roles and Team Strategy">

Fart With Your Friends is easier to manage when each player has a clear role. The movement itself is shared, but the decision-making does not need to be chaotic. One player can focus on propulsion and directional control while the other tracks hazards, timing, and route changes.

These roles can change when the route demands it. A player who handled the previous section may not have the best view for the next one. The team should switch responsibilities when visibility or positioning changes.

  <Card title="Controller">

| Team Problem | Likely Cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| The route repeatedly moves backward | No one confirms the intended direction | Pause and name the next target |
| Players argue over inputs | Multiple callers issue commands | Select one caller for the section |
| The team overshoots hazards | Inputs are held too long | Use shorter pushes and planned pauses |
| Progress stops near an obstacle | The angle is not being reassessed | Re-center before applying more movement |
| A checkpoint is reached but lost quickly | The team rushes into the next area | Stop, scout, and plan the next segment |

A good team call should describe both direction and intensity. “Right” identifies the route, but “light right” communicates how much movement is appropriate. “Forward, then stop” is more useful than repeating “go” while the team approaches a spike.

<Callout type="info" title="Communication Upgrade">
Use two-part commands for difficult sections: direction plus intensity. Examples include “light left,” “slow forward,” and “right, then stop.” This reduces ambiguity when the route changes quickly.
</Callout>

If the team fails several times at the same obstacle, change the process instead of repeating the same attempt. Switch the caller, reduce movement strength, or approach from a different angle. Repetition only helps when the team learns something from each failure.

</Section>

<Section icon={Trophy} title="Practice Plan and Progress Checklist">

The fastest way to improve is to practice one mechanic at a time. New players often try to solve movement, route reading, and communication simultaneously. A structured practice plan makes each skill easier to recognize.

Start with open sections and learn how much movement a short input creates. Next, practice stopping before a hazard. Finally, combine direction calls with checkpoint planning. The goal is consistent control, not simply completing one section through luck.

| Practice Phase | Main Objective | Success Indicator |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Learn basic propulsion | The team can move without immediate overcorrection |
| Phase 2 | Practice stopping | Players can pause before spikes or poison areas |
| Phase 3 | Add route calls | One player directs while the other scouts |
| Phase 4 | Use checkpoints | The team plans each section from a stable position |
| Phase 5 | Handle failure | Players reset calmly instead of repeating uncontrolled inputs |

Spend a few attempts focusing only on controlled stopping. Once the team can stop safely, directional changes and hazard navigation become easier to manage.

Use the following routine during a focused session:

1. **Observe the section** before applying movement.
2. **Name the next safe position** rather than the entire route.
3. **Apply a short push** and watch the resulting angle.
4. **Stop before the hazard** whenever possible.
5. **Review the mistake** after failure and change one variable on the next attempt.

This approach keeps practice measurable. If the team fails, identify whether the problem was excessive force, unclear communication, poor route reading, or a late correction. Changing one factor at a time makes improvement easier to track.
