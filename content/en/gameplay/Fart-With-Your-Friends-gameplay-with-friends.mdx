Fart With Your Friends is best approached as a cooperative obstacle course where two players must coordinate movement instead of competing for individual performance. The central challenge is controlling shared momentum through hazards, turns, bouncing sections, and narrow routes. One player may appear to guide the movement, but both players need to communicate because a late correction can send the team into a spike or back toward an earlier section.

The most important habit is to establish a simple communication system before the run begins. Use short calls such as “left,” “right,” “stop,” “slow,” and “forward.” Long explanations are difficult to follow while the characters are moving, especially when the route includes bounce surfaces or hazards on both sides.

| Communication Call | Meaning | Best Use |
|---|---|---|
| Left or right | Begin a directional correction | Turning around corners or aligning with a route |
| Stop | Reduce or end the current input | Preventing an overshoot near a hazard |
| Slow | Use a lighter movement input | Narrow paths and checkpoint approaches |
| Forward | Continue along the intended route | Straight sections with visible clearance |
| Reset | Accept a failed position and recover | Returning to a checkpoint after getting stuck |

A good team also decides who has primary control during each section. Switching control repeatedly can create conflicting inputs. Instead, one player should lead the movement while the other serves as a spotter. The spotter watches for spikes, poison, walls, and incorrect routes, then gives concise instructions.

<Callout type="tip" title="Communication Rule">
Use one directional command at a time. Calling “left, right, stop” together can cause the lead player to overcorrect and lose control.
</Callout>

<Section icon={Target} title="Movement and Timing Strategy">

Movement in Fart With Your Friends rewards controlled inputs rather than constant pressure. The cooperative character can move farther than expected when both players continue pushing in the same direction, so holding a direction too long may turn a successful approach into a collision. The safest method is to divide every obstacle into three phases: approach, alignment, and release.

During the approach, move toward the obstacle without rushing. Once the team is aligned with the safe route, reduce the input and watch how the characters respond. Near the hazard, release or change direction only when the lead player has identified the next safe surface. This rhythm helps prevent the repeated left-right corrections that often send the team backward.

| Phase | Team Goal | Recommended Input |
|---|---|---|
| Approach | Reach the obstacle safely | Moderate movement |
| Alignment | Center the characters with the route | Small corrections |
| Release | Avoid overshooting the landing area | Stop or light input |
| Recovery | Reposition after a mistake | Calm, single-direction adjustment |

Bounce sections require especially careful timing. A bounce can change the team’s position faster than ordinary movement, and the next correction may need to happen immediately after landing. The spotter should identify the next direction before the bounce occurs, so the lead player does not have to make a decision while reacting to the landing.

Small movements are also useful when one player needs to avoid a hazard while the other manages the route. The goal is not to move as quickly as possible. The goal is to preserve a stable position long enough to identify the next action.

<Section icon={Map} title="Step-by-Step Cooperative Route Method">

The most reliable way to handle difficult sections is to turn each obstacle into a short route plan. This approach is useful when the team reaches spikes, narrow platforms, bounce points, or an area where the correct direction is unclear.

<Steps>
  <Step num="1" title="Identify the Safe Direction">
    Before moving, locate the visible route and check which sides contain spikes, poison, walls, or drop-offs. The spotter should name the safe direction clearly.
  </Step>
  <Step num="2" title="Choose the Lead Player">
    Give primary control to the player with the clearest view or better timing. The second player should avoid issuing competing movement commands.
  </Step>
  <Step num="3" title="Approach Slowly">
    Move toward the obstacle with moderate pressure. Stop rushing once the team is close enough to make a precise correction.
  </Step>
  <Step num="4" title="Make One Correction">
    Use a single left, right, or forward adjustment. Watch the movement before adding another command, especially near spikes and narrow routes.
  </Step>
  <Step num="5" title="Confirm the Checkpoint">
    Once the team reaches a checkpoint, pause briefly and agree on the next route. If the position becomes impossible, use the checkpoint rather than forcing repeated movement.
  </Step>
</Steps>

This method prevents a common failure pattern: both players react to the same mistake at the same time. When each player provides a different correction, the team may launch in the wrong direction or return to an earlier section. A clear leader and a single spotter call make the movement easier to understand.

| Situation | Primary Risk | Correct Response |
|---|---|---|
| Spikes on one side | Turning too sharply | Use a small correction away from the hazard |
| Hazards on both sides | Panic movement | Slow down and follow one call |
| Bounce surface | Landing misalignment | Plan the next direction before contact |
| Stuck against a wall | Repeated conflicting inputs | Stop, reposition, or reset to the checkpoint |
| Wrong route | Wasted movement | Confirm the path before continuing |

If the team reaches an area that appears impossible, check whether the route has been reversed. Cooperative obstacle games can make a wrong direction look temporarily successful, especially when a bounce or push carries the characters farther than expected. Confirm the intended route before spending several attempts on the same mistake.

<Section icon={Shield} title="Hazards, Checkpoints, and Recovery">

Hazards are not only tests of movement; they also test the team’s ability to recover from mistakes. Spikes, poison areas, walls, and narrow surfaces each require a different response. Rushing after a failed attempt often creates another collision, while a deliberate reset gives the team a better chance to repeat the section with improved timing.

Checkpoints should be treated as strategic milestones. They divide a difficult run into smaller practice sections and reduce the cost of mistakes. When the team reaches one, remember the route immediately before it and identify the next visible hazard. This makes it easier to restart with a plan instead of improvising.

| Hazard Type | Warning Sign | Recovery Priority |
|---|---|---|
| Spike | Sharp obstacle near the route | Stop unnecessary lateral movement |
| Poison area | Unsafe surface or colored hazard zone | Keep the team aligned with the safe path |
| Wall or edge | Limited room for correction | Use light inputs and avoid repeated pushes |
| Bounce section | Sudden change in position | Prepare the next direction before landing |
| Wrong-way route | Progress begins reversing | Pause and verify the intended direction |

When the team becomes trapped, do not assume that more force will solve the problem. Repeated pushing can move the characters farther from the route or launch them into a hazard. First, stop the current input. Next, identify whether the team can return safely. If not, accept the reset and use the checkpoint as a practice point.

A reset is not a failure of the overall run. It is a way to remove a bad position and restore a known starting point. Teams that reset early often spend less time than teams that continue fighting an unstable position.

<Section icon={Trophy} title="Practice Plan and Frequently Asked Questions">

Improvement comes from repeating specific obstacle types instead of restarting without a plan. After each failed attempt, identify the exact cause: excessive movement, a late call, an incorrect route, or a poor bounce landing. Then change only one part of the strategy on the next attempt. This makes it easier to tell which adjustment helped.

A useful practice cycle is to complete one safe section, pause at the checkpoint, and then focus on the next obstacle. If the team fails repeatedly at the same point, switch roles so the other player can lead. A new viewpoint may make the route easier to read, but role changes should happen during a reset rather than in the middle of movement.

| Practice Goal | What to Watch | Improvement Signal |
|---|---|---|
| Better control | Fewer unnecessary direction changes | The team stays aligned longer |
| Better timing | Earlier hazard calls | Corrections happen before contact |
| Better teamwork | Clear lead and spotter roles | Fewer conflicting commands |
| Better recovery | Faster checkpoint resets | Less time spent in unstable positions |
| Better route knowledge | Recognition of wrong turns | The team stops reversing direction |

The best Fart With Your Friends gameplay with friends sessions are not necessarily the fastest. They are the sessions where both players understand the route, communicate clearly, and recover from mistakes without creating new ones. Treat every failed attempt as route information, then apply one focused change.
