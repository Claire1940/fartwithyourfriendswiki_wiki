<Section icon={Map} title="Fart With Your Friends how to win: Core Movement">

The most reliable answer to **Fart With Your Friends how to win** is to treat every fart burst as a shared movement tool. The team is not simply racing forward. Each player changes the group’s position, speed, and spacing, so an individual action can help everyone or create a wipe.

The strongest teams use short, deliberate bursts. They check the next opening, agree on the direction, and then release movement together. When the route becomes narrow, players should reduce their output rather than trying to force maximum distance.

| Movement Situation | Recommended Action | Common Mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Open space | Use a measured burst toward the next landmark | Spending all available gas immediately |
| Narrow passage | Tap movement and leave room to correct | Holding the burst too long |
| Turn or corner | Wait for the caller, then move together | Turning different directions |
| Hazard nearby | Stop, align, and use a small push | Panicking and pushing into danger |
| Recovery after a miss | Re-center before moving again | Trying to repair movement with random bursts |

  <Card title="Shared Direction">

<Section icon={Target} title="Build a Reliable Team Callout System">

Clear communication is more valuable than fast reactions. A team should use short commands that describe the next action, not long explanations during a dangerous section. One player can act as the caller while the others confirm their position and follow the timing.

Useful commands include “hold,” “slow,” “send,” “back,” and “stop.” The exact vocabulary does not matter as much as consistency. If “send” means a strong group movement, it should not be used for a tiny adjustment later in the same run.

| Callout | Meaning | Best Use |
|---|---|---|
| Hold | Do not release gas yet | Waiting for alignment or a hazard cycle |
| Slow | Use a light burst only | Narrow routes and precision turns |
| Send | Commit to a stronger movement | Clear openings and long safe sections |
| Back | Move away from the current danger | Recovering from a bad angle |
| Stop | Cancel the next movement | Preventing an accidental push |
| Ready | Confirm position and attention | Preparing for a coordinated action |

The caller should not constantly issue commands. Too many instructions create confusion and cause players to react before the important cue. A good pattern is:

- Identify the next safe area.
- Confirm which players need to move.
- Give one clear command.
- Wait for the group to settle.
- Repeat only when the route changes.

<Callout type="warning" title="Avoid Mixed Signals">
Do not combine “go,” “stop,” and “wait” in the same moment. If the team receives conflicting instructions, pause and re-establish one caller before continuing.
</Callout>

A second player can watch the rear of the group and warn about characters drifting away from the intended route. This is especially useful when the camera angle or level geometry makes it difficult for the caller to see everyone at once.

The team should also agree on who takes responsibility for uncertain decisions. If nobody owns the call, several players may attempt to lead simultaneously. That usually produces overlapping bursts and poor positioning.

</Section>

<Section icon={Swords} title="Step-by-Step Winning Method">

Use the following routine at the start of each section, not only after a failed attempt. Repeating the same setup makes successful movement easier to reproduce and helps the group identify which decision caused a mistake.

<Steps>
  <Step num="1" title="Scan the Route">
    Before moving, identify the next platform, turn, opening, or hazard. Do not begin with a large burst simply because the route appears open.
  </Step>
  <Step num="2" title="Set the Formation">
    Move players into a workable position with enough space to correct direction. Characters that are already near an edge should wait for a safer angle.
  </Step>
  <Step num="3" title="Choose the Burst Strength">
    Decide whether the section needs a light adjustment, a steady push, or a committed send. Use one shared strength instead of several unrelated inputs.
  </Step>
  <Step num="4" title="Move on One Cue">
    The caller gives the command, and the team releases movement together. Players should avoid adding extra gas unless the caller changes the plan.
  </Step>
  <Step num="5" title="Stabilize Before Repeating">
    Once the group reaches the next safe area, stop and check the route again. Immediate follow-up movement is a common cause of overshooting.
  </Step>
</Steps>

| Phase | Team Question | Success Condition |
|---|---|---|
| Scan | Where is the next safe position? | Everyone knows the target |
| Formation | Are players aligned and spaced? | No one is trapped against an edge |
| Selection | How much movement is required? | The group agrees on burst strength |
| Execution | Who gives the cue? | One synchronized action occurs |
| Stabilization | Are we ready for the next section? | The group is settled before continuing |

<Callout type="success" title="Winning Pattern">
A successful run usually looks controlled rather than spectacular: scan, align, burst, stabilize, and repeat. Consistency beats risky speed when the route becomes technical.
</Callout>

If the group misses a turn, do not immediately attempt a second large push. First identify whether the team is too far forward, too far back, or facing the wrong direction. A small correction often creates a better recovery position than another full-strength movement.

</Section>

<Section icon={Shield} title="Hazards, Turns, and Recovery Decisions">

Most failures happen when the team treats a hazard like an ordinary stretch of open space. Dangerous areas require a different rhythm: less movement, more observation, and a clear exit plan. The group should know where it wants to stop after passing the hazard.

Turns are equally important. A team that reaches a corner with too much momentum may be unable to rotate safely. Begin slowing before the turn rather than trying to correct the angle after crossing it.

| Problem | Why It Happens | Better Response |
|---|---|---|
| Overshooting an opening | The team used a strong burst too late | Slow before the opening and commit earlier |
| Pushing into a wall | Players moved without checking alignment | Stop, rotate the formation, then use a light burst |
| Splitting the group | Players followed different directions | Return to one caller and use a shared target |
| Losing control near a hazard | Gas was released continuously | Pause between bursts and preserve correction space |
| Repeating the same mistake | The team changed actions without reviewing the cause | Name the failed decision before trying again |

Use the following priority order when recovering:

1. Protect the players who are still in a safe position.
2. Stop unnecessary movement.
3. Identify the nearest stable area.
4. Move the group toward that area with small corrections.
5. Resume normal pacing only after everyone is aligned.

<Callout type="warning" title="Recovery Rule">
Do not sacrifice a stable position to rescue a player who is already drifting into danger. Stabilize the main group first, then attempt a controlled recovery if the route allows it.
</Callout>

When a teammate makes an accidental burst, avoid blaming them during the active sequence. The immediate goal is to prevent a second error. After the group is safe, discuss whether the problem came from unclear timing, poor spacing, or an incorrect route call.

| Recovery Type | First Action | Follow-Up |
|---|---|---|
| Too far forward | Stop movement | Wait for the route to clear |
| Too far backward | Re-align the group | Use a measured forward burst |
| Wrong direction | Establish the correct target | Rotate before moving |
| Group separated | Protect the larger cluster | Reconnect using short bursts |
| Hazard contact imminent | Cancel extra inputs | Move away only when the path is clear |

</Section>
