The most useful communication vocabulary is simple:

- **Go**: begin or resume movement.
- **Brake**: reduce speed immediately.
- **Hold**: maintain the current position.
- **Left** or **right**: make a small directional adjustment.
- **Wait**: do not enter the next hazard yet.
- **Push**: apply coordinated forward pressure.
- **Checkpoint**: pause briefly after reaching a progress marker.

Short calls are better than explanations during a hazard. Save longer discussions for the recovery area or after the run.

These roles are especially useful in sections with narrow lanes, moving hazards, or opposing currents. The route caller should avoid giving commands too late. A warning before the hazard is more valuable than a reaction after contact.

| Course Situation | Primary Role | Team Response |
|---|---|---|
| Open straightaway | Momentum Lead | Build speed gradually |
| Spike-filled passage | Route Caller | Announce entry and braking point |
| Strong current | Stabilizer | Make small corrections toward the center |
| Narrow platform | All players | Reduce sudden inputs and maintain spacing |
| Missed checkpoint | Recovery Player | Confirm the restart point before moving |
| Final approach | Route Caller | Replace panic with short, timed commands |

### How to Avoid Conflicting Inputs

Conflicting movement is one of the biggest causes of failed attempts. If multiple players issue commands at the same time, the group may accelerate when it should brake or turn into a hazard while trying to correct away from it.

Use this communication pattern:

1. The route caller identifies the next hazard.
2. The caller gives one command.
3. Other players acknowledge briefly.
4. The team makes small corrections.
5. The caller confirms when the section is clear.

Avoid constant commentary. Repeated commands can make players overcorrect, especially when the course requires a short pause before the next movement burst.

The demo’s challenge comes from managing momentum under pressure. Moving quickly is useful on safe stretches, but speed must be balanced against the time required to brake, redirect, or recover from a collision.

<Callout type="info" title="Route Reading">
Look beyond the obstacle directly in front of you. The best braking point is often determined by the hazard after the next one, not the obstacle currently on screen.
</Callout>

### Braking and Momentum

Use gradual movement whenever the course is unfamiliar. A short burst followed by a controlled brake gives the team more options than holding maximum speed through every section.

| Movement Pattern | Best Use | Main Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Slow advance | Tight platforms and unfamiliar hazards | Progress may feel cautious |
| Short burst | Open gaps between obstacles | Early acceleration can overshoot |
| Burst then brake | Approaches requiring precise entry | Braking too late causes collisions |
| Hold position | Timing-based hazards and currents | Waiting too long can lose alignment |
| Coordinated push | Sections requiring group momentum | Conflicting inputs can destabilize the team |

### Handling Currents

When a current pulls the group away from the intended route, avoid dramatic corrections. Several small adjustments are easier to control than one large input. The team should aim for the center of the safe lane whenever possible.

A useful current sequence is:

- Reduce unnecessary speed.
- Identify the direction of the pull.
- Call the correction before entering the strongest part.
- Make a small adjustment toward the safe center.
- Brake if the group begins drifting too far.
- Resume movement only after the formation is stable.

### Managing Checkpoints

Checkpoints should change the team’s priorities. Before reaching one, focus on survival and positioning. After reaching one, take a brief pause to reset communication and confirm the next route.

Do not immediately rush away from a checkpoint if the upcoming section is unclear. A few seconds of planning can prevent a longer restart.

A short practice plan helps new players improve without turning every attempt into a rushed speedrun. Start by learning control distance, then add communication, and only afterward optimize completion speed.

| Practice Round | Focus | Success Measure |
|---|---|---|

| Round 4 | Checkpoint consistency | The group reaches progress markers reliably |
| Round 5 | Speed refinement | Faster movement does not create repeated collisions |

### Suggested Improvement Loop

After each attempt, discuss only three points:

- What caused the first major mistake?
- Which command arrived too late or was unclear?
- What single adjustment should the team try next?

This keeps feedback useful and prevents the session from becoming a debate after every collision. If one player is consistently better at route reading, let that player call the next attempt. Roles can change after the group becomes comfortable with the course.
