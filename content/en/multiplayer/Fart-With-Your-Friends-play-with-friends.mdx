<Section icon={Users} title="Fart With Your Friends play with friends: Co-op Basics">

Fart With Your Friends is best approached as a communication-focused co-op platforming game. When playing with friends, success depends less on individual speed and more on synchronized movement, controlled fart bursts, and clear calls before each obstacle. The main challenge is keeping the group together while navigating platforms, checkpoints, environmental hazards, and sections that punish rushed movement.

The most reliable setup uses a host, a caller, a movement lead, and a player who watches for hazards. These roles do not need to be permanent. Rotate them whenever a section becomes difficult or when one player has a better view of the route.

<Section icon={Target} title="Co-op Setup Before the First Attempt">

A strong session begins before the first difficult obstacle. Decide who will host, confirm that everyone can hear voice chat, and agree on a simple callout system. This prevents confusion when a player needs to stop, wait for a teammate, or warn the group about a hazard.

The host should also explain how the team will handle mistakes. The cleanest rule is to stop at the next safe point, identify what failed, and retry with one change. Repeating the same jump with the same timing usually creates frustration without improving the result.

| Setup Task | Recommended Action | Why It Helps |
|---|---|---|
| Host selection | Choose the player with the most stable connection | Reduces avoidable interruptions during group movement |
| Voice chat | Confirm every player can hear the caller | Makes timing and hazard warnings easier |
| Role assignment | Pick a caller, lead, and hazard watcher | Gives each player a clear responsibility |
| Retry rule | Change one timing or route decision per attempt | Makes failures easier to diagnose |
| Checkpoint rule | Regroup before continuing | Prevents players from entering the next section alone |

  <Step num="1" title="Create the Session">

  <Step num="2" title="Choose the Caller">
    Assign one player to control the group’s timing. The caller should announce when to wait, move, slow down, or prepare for a jump.

A consistent vocabulary is more valuable than detailed commentary. “Ready,” “wait,” “go,” “slow,” and “stop” are enough for most cooperative movement sequences.

| Callout | Meaning | When to Use It |
|---|---|---|
| Ready | Everyone is positioned | Before a synchronized jump |
| Wait | Do not trigger movement yet | When a teammate is still recovering |
| Go | Begin the planned movement | After the route is clear |
| Slow | Reduce the next burst | Near a narrow platform |
| Reset | Return to the last safe position | After the group loses formation |

<Section icon={Map} title="Timing Jumps, Platforms, and Fart Bursts">

The central co-op skill is timing. Players need to treat each fart burst as a movement tool rather than a button to use continuously. Short bursts allow the team to correct position, while longer bursts can create distance when the route is open. The correct choice depends on the platform width, the next landing area, and whether teammates are ready to follow.

For most difficult sections, use a three-phase rhythm: line up, call the move, then correct the landing. Do not let every player experiment at the same time. One player can test the route while the others watch and prepare to copy the successful timing.

| Situation | Safer Approach | Common Mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Wide platform | Use a moderate burst and adjust after landing | Continuing movement after reaching safety |
| Narrow platform | Use short bursts with a pause between them | Holding the burst too long |
| Group jump | Wait until all players are aligned | Starting before the slowest player is ready |
| Unknown route | Let the movement lead test first | Sending the entire group forward blindly |
| Recovery | Stop and regain position | Trying to force another jump immediately |

<Callout type="warning" title="Avoid Panic Bursts">
When a player misses a landing, avoid pressing movement repeatedly without a plan. Panic bursts can push teammates into the same hazard and make recovery harder.
</Callout>

A useful sequence for a difficult platform is:

- Move into a stable starting position.
- Confirm that the next landing area is clear.
- Announce the burst length or direction.
- Move together when the caller gives the signal.
- Stop once the first player reaches a safe position.
- Let the remaining players copy the timing.

If the team keeps overshooting, shorten the burst and add a pause before the landing. If players fall short, start from a more forward position or use a slightly longer burst. Change only one variable per attempt so the team learns what actually worked.

</Section>

<Section icon={Shield} title="Checkpoints, Hazards, and Monster Sections">

Checkpoints should shape the way your group plays. Once the team reaches one, stop and confirm that everyone is ready before continuing. A checkpoint is not only progress protection; it is also a communication reset. Players can review the route, switch roles, and decide who will lead the next attempt.

Hazard sections require more discipline than ordinary platforming. The group should move in a compact formation, avoid unnecessary bursts, and keep track of where the threat is located. When a monster or moving hazard enters the route, the caller should prioritize survival over speed.

| Challenge Type | Team Priority | Recommended Response |
|---|---|---|
| Moving hazard | Maintain spacing | Follow the caller and avoid sudden direction changes |
| Monster section | Control noise and movement | Use deliberate bursts and watch the threat’s position |
| Narrow route | Preserve alignment | Move one player at a time if necessary |
| Missed timing | Recover safely | Stop, regroup, and retry from the last stable position |
| Multiple hazards | Maintain an escape option | Do not commit the full group without a fallback route |

<Callout type="success" title="Checkpoint Habit">
After reaching a checkpoint, pause long enough to confirm the whole group is present. Continuing with one player missing often creates unnecessary deaths and repeated sections.
</Callout>

During monster encounters, avoid treating the route like a race. One player should watch the threat while another calls movement. If the team has to choose between a fast route and a safer route, use the option that keeps more players together. A coordinated retreat is often better than separating the group across multiple platforms.

</Section>

| Problem | Likely Cause | Practical Fix |
|---|---|---|

| Monster section collapses | Players panic after a warning | Use one hazard watcher and one retreat call |
| Repeated identical failures | No attempt is being analyzed | Change one variable before retrying |

<Callout type="tip" title="Review the Last Attempt">
After a failed run, ask one question: did the team fail because of timing, positioning, or communication? Fix that category first instead of changing the entire route.
</Callout>

A reliable group progression loop looks like this:

1. Reach a safe platform.
2. Confirm the entire group is ready.
3. Identify the next hazard or landing point.
4. Choose the player who will test or lead the movement.
5. Execute the burst on the caller’s signal.
6. Stop, regroup, and repeat from the next safe position.

This method is especially useful when the team is dealing with limited attempts, repeated deaths, or a route that includes several consecutive jumps. The goal is not simply to move faster. The goal is to reduce unplanned movement and make every attempt informative.
