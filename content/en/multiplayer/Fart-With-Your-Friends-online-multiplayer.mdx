<Section icon={Target} title="How to coordinate movement and propulsion">

Successful teams separate navigation from correction. One player should identify the route and announce the next direction, while the other watches the team’s position relative to spikes, walls, and dangerous edges. This division makes the shared movement system easier to manage.

Do not treat every obstacle as a problem that requires immediate movement. Some sections are safer when the pair pauses, lines up, and waits for a clear opening. The gameplay example shows repeated attempts where fast corrections cause the team to reverse direction or collide with hazards. A controlled pause often creates more progress than another rushed input.

A useful communication pattern is “prepare, move, stop.” First, the route caller identifies the direction. Next, the team applies a short input. Finally, both players stop long enough to check the new position. This rhythm is especially valuable after a bounce, a launch, or a turn around a wall.

If the team begins moving backward, do not immediately add more force. First determine whether the players are holding conflicting directions or whether the pair has passed the correct angle. A brief reset can prevent a small mistake from becoming a full restart.

<Callout type="warning" title="Avoid Mixed Inputs">
When one player calls for a left turn and the other continues pushing right, the pair can lose control near hazards. Agree on one direction before applying force.
</Callout>

<Section icon={Shield} title="Obstacle and hazard strategy">

Hazards should be handled according to their position, not simply their appearance. Spikes beside the route require lateral control, while spikes above or below the team demand careful timing. Walls and trees can restrict movement, making a large push more dangerous than a small correction.

The safest approach is to identify the hazard that can end the attempt immediately, then manage the team’s position around it. Do not focus so heavily on the next checkpoint that you ignore a nearby obstacle. Reaching a checkpoint has value only if the team arrives with enough control to continue.

| Hazard Type | Common Mistake | Safer Technique |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Side spikes | Holding a direction too long | Tap toward the open side |
| Red obstacles | Entering without alignment | Stop, line up, then push |
| Poison spikes | Watching the route but not the edge | Assign one player to hazard watch |
| Walls and trees | Trying to force through | Reposition before applying propulsion |
| Bounce points | Using maximum momentum | Approach slowly and launch only when aligned |

When the route becomes difficult, divide the obstacle into smaller decisions. First, find the safe side. Second, align the pair with that side. Third, apply enough movement to pass the hazard. Finally, stop and reassess. This method limits the number of variables changing at once.

A failed attempt should also provide information. Note whether the team was too fast, too far to one side, or moving in the wrong direction. Repeating the same input without changing the approach usually produces the same result. A short discussion after each mistake can improve the next attempt.

<Callout type="success" title="Hazard Rule">
If a section contains spikes on both sides, prioritize alignment over speed. Reaching the opening slowly is usually more useful than arriving quickly with unstable momentum.
</Callout>

<Section icon={Zap} title="Step-by-step multiplayer route method">

Use this process whenever the route contains multiple hazards, bounce points, or a difficult checkpoint. It is designed to keep both players focused on one decision at a time.

<Steps>
  <Step num="1" title="Choose the route caller">
    Decide which player will announce directions for the current obstacle. The second player should focus on position, nearby hazards, and whether the pair is drifting.
  </Step>
  <Step num="2" title="Stop before the obstacle">
    Avoid entering a difficult section while moving unpredictably. Pause long enough to identify the open side, the next checkpoint, and the direction of the first correction.
  </Step>
  <Step num="3" title="Apply a short input">
    Move in the announced direction with a controlled burst. Do not keep pushing automatically after the pair begins to turn or launch.
  </Step>
  <Step num="4" title="Confirm alignment">
    Check whether the team is facing the next opening. If the pair is too close to a spike or wall, correct position before attempting another push.
  </Step>
  <Step num="5" title="Secure the checkpoint">
    Once the team reaches a checkpoint, pause and confirm progress. Use the checkpoint as the new starting reference for the next obstacle.
  </Step>
</Steps>

| Step | Team Question | Desired Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Who is calling the route? | One clear direction source |

| 3 | How much movement is needed? | A short, controlled input |
| 4 | Are we aligned? | The pair faces the safe opening |
| 5 | Did we secure progress? | The checkpoint becomes the new reference |

This method also helps when the team reaches a confusing area. Instead of guessing, return to the last confirmed position and rebuild the route from there. If a reset is necessary, use it intentionally to return to a known checkpoint rather than continuing with poor alignment.

<Callout type="info" title="Checkpoint Planning">
Treat every checkpoint as a staging area. After reaching one, pause, review the next hazard, and change roles if the current caller is struggling.
</Callout>

A strong team does not need constant conversation. It needs useful communication at the right moments. Long explanations during a dangerous section can create hesitation, while short commands keep reactions clear. Discuss the plan before moving, then use brief calls during the attempt.

For practice sessions, focus on one skill at a time:

- **Movement control:** Learn how quickly the pair changes direction.
- **Hazard awareness:** Identify which obstacle requires the most attention.
- **Timing:** Practice stopping before a turn or bounce.
- **Recovery:** Learn when to reset instead of forcing a bad position.
- **Communication:** Use consistent words for direction, speed, and stopping.

<Callout type="tip" title="Practice Advice">
