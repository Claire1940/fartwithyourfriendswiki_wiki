<Section icon={Users} title="Fart With Your Friends multiplayer basics">

Fart With Your Friends multiplayer is built around shared movement, where two players must coordinate pushes, turns, jumps, and hazard timing. The central challenge is not simply reaching the next platform. Both players must agree on direction while reacting to spikes, walls, gaps, and other obstacles.

The most reliable approach is to give one player primary control during each movement sequence. The second player should provide short, clear instructions and avoid making sudden counter-movements unless the route changes. This reduces the back-and-forth motion that can send both characters into a hazard or back toward the previous checkpoint.

<Section icon={Target} title="Step-by-step co-op movement method">

A repeatable movement routine helps prevent panic when the route becomes crowded with spikes or narrow turns. The goal is to separate planning from execution. Stop whenever possible, identify the next safe position, and then move with one agreed instruction.

<Steps>
  <Step num="1" title="Choose the active driver">
    Decide which player is responsible for the next movement. The active driver should make the main directional input, while the other player watches the route and gives concise instructions.
  </Step>
  <Step num="2" title="Identify the next safe surface">
    Before pushing forward, locate the next platform, wall edge, checkpoint, or open area. Do not move simply because the characters are facing the correct direction.
  </Step>
  <Step num="3" title="Apply a small input">
    Start with a light push rather than holding the direction for too long. Small corrections make it easier to avoid spikes and recover from a bad angle.
  </Step>
  <Step num="4" title="Confirm the position">
    Once the characters reach a safer area, stop and reassess. Confirm whether the next move requires a turn, a bounce, a slow approach, or a deliberate reset.
  </Step>
  <Step num="5" title="Repeat after each checkpoint">
    Treat every checkpoint as a new setup. Reassign the driver if needed, then use the same identify, push, and confirm routine.
  </Step>
</Steps>

| Step | Team objective | Best communication |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Establish control | “You drive this part.” |
| 2 | Find the route | “Next safe area is left.” |
| 3 | Start movement | “Light push.” |
| 4 | Avoid overcorrection | “Stop there.” |
| 5 | Continue from safety | “Checkpoint reached.” |

<Callout type="warning" title="Avoid Input Conflicts">
The most damaging mistake is having both players make opposite decisions at the same time. If the route is uncertain, stop first, then assign one player to handle the next movement.
</Callout>

A useful rule is to communicate before acting, not during a panic moment. “Go right” is easier to follow than a long explanation after the characters have already started drifting left. If a player misses an instruction, pause at the next safe opportunity and reset the plan.

</Section>

<Section icon={Shield} title="Hazard control and checkpoint recovery">

Hazards are easier to manage when the team treats each obstacle as a timing problem instead of a race. The gameplay footage emphasizes repeated attempts around spikes, walls, turns, and difficult sections where excessive movement causes the characters to slide backward or launch into danger.

The safest pattern is to approach obstacles slowly, use the environment to stabilize your position, and avoid making several directional changes in quick succession. When a mistake occurs, focus on returning to a controllable position rather than immediately forcing progress.

| Hazard type | Risk | Safer technique |
|---|---|---|
| Spikes | A long push can launch the team into damage | Approach slowly and stop before the hazard |
| Poison or red obstacles | A poor angle can block the route | Keep the hazard on one side and make small corrections |
| Walls and edges | Uncontrolled bouncing can reverse progress | Use the wall as a reference point |
| Narrow turns | Opposite inputs can create sideways drift | Name the turn before moving |
| Backtracking | Panic can send the team toward the start | Stop and locate the latest checkpoint |

<Callout type="info" title="Checkpoint Mindset">
A checkpoint is more valuable than a few extra seconds of progress. Once one is reached, use it as the team’s new reference point and avoid repeating an earlier route without a clear reason.
</Callout>

When a run becomes unstable, use this recovery order:

- Stop applying unnecessary movement.
- Identify whether the characters are moving forward, backward, or sideways.
- Check whether the next safe position is reachable.
- If the position is unsalvageable, allow a reset back to the checkpoint.
- Resume with a new driver and a simpler instruction.

This method prevents a common multiplayer problem: continuing a bad attempt because the team has already invested time in it. A controlled reset can be faster than several frantic corrections.

</Section>

<Section icon={Gauge} title="Timing, pacing, and advanced coordination">

Once the basic movement routine feels comfortable, the next improvement is pacing. Faster inputs are not automatically better. The team should adjust movement speed to the amount of space available, the distance to the next hazard, and the amount of control each player currently has.

Use fast movement only across open sections where a mistake is unlikely to cause immediate damage. Slow down near spikes, narrow platforms, corners, and areas where the route is difficult to read. The change in pace should be announced so both players understand why the movement is becoming more cautious.

<Callout type="success" title="Advanced Tip">
The strongest teams do not try to eliminate every mistake. They make mistakes easier to recover from by slowing down near hazards and preserving a stable position.
</Callout>

A good partner also knows when not to speak. If the active driver is already aligned with the correct route, extra instructions can create hesitation. Use specific calls only when the direction, timing, or hazard position changes.

For difficult sections, divide the route into small objectives:

- Reach the next wall or platform.
- Stop before the hazard.
- Turn only after the route is confirmed.
- Move through the opening.
- Pause at the next checkpoint.

Breaking a long obstacle course into these smaller targets makes progress easier to measure and reduces frustration during repeated attempts.
