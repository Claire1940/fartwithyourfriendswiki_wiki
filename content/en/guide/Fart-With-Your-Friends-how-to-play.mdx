<Section icon={Map} title="Fart With Your Friends how to play: Core Movement">

Fart With Your Friends is a cooperative obstacle-course experience where players work together to move through hazards using timed, directional fart propulsion. The central challenge is not simply reaching the next platform. Both players must agree on the direction, control the strength of each movement, and react quickly when the course changes.

The most reliable opening strategy is to treat movement like steering a shared vehicle. One player should call the direction, while the other confirms the input before applying force. This prevents the common problem of both players making different corrections and sending the team into a spike or back toward an earlier section.

**Video Highlights:**

- Cooperative movement depends on clear left, right, and forward calls.
- Short propulsion bursts are safer near spikes and narrow obstacles.
- Checkpoints provide valuable recovery points during difficult sections.
- Players can lose progress when conflicting inputs send the team backward.
- Patient coordination is more effective than repeated emergency corrections.

The strongest beginner habit is to separate steering from propulsion. Do not change direction and apply maximum movement at the same moment unless the route is wide and safe. Instead, line up the shared character, make a small adjustment, and then use a controlled burst.

<Section icon={Users} title="Co-op Coordination and Communication">

Successful teams use simple, repeatable commands instead of long explanations during a dangerous section. Words such as “left,” “right,” “slow,” “stop,” and “forward” are easier to follow than several instructions delivered at once. The goal is to make every movement call clear before the next burst begins.

When both players are actively pushing in different directions, the shared character can drift, reverse, or launch into a hazard. This is especially costly around spike walls, poison areas, and sections where a bounce must be aimed toward a specific landing point.

| Callout | Meaning | Best Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Forward | Move toward the next route section | Open paths and checkpoint approaches |
| Left or right | Adjust the travel direction | Turns, walls, and angled platforms |
| Slow | Reduce movement intensity | Narrow routes and hazard entrances |
| Stop | Pause before the next input | Route checks and timing puzzles |
| Reset | Accept a failed position and recover | When the team is trapped or misaligned |

Use a caller rotation when one player becomes overwhelmed. A controller who is struggling to judge the route may perform better when focused only on input timing. Likewise, a caller who cannot see the next obstacle should let the other player lead for that section.

A useful communication pattern is “direction, strength, stop.” For example, one player calls “right, small, stop.” This gives the controller a destination, an intensity level, and a clear end point. The same pattern works when approaching a checkpoint or bouncing from a wall.

<Callout type="warning" title="Avoid Conflicting Inputs">
Two players making opposite corrections can create more danger than the original mistake. If the route becomes unclear, pause and agree on the next direction before moving again.
</Callout>

<Section icon={Target} title="Step-by-Step Obstacle Course Method">

The course becomes easier when each obstacle is divided into preparation, movement, and recovery. Rushing directly into a hazard often creates a chain reaction: the first correction pushes the team into a second obstacle, and the resulting panic sends the character back toward the previous section.

<Steps>
  <Step num="1" title="Read the Route">
    Before applying force, identify the next safe direction, the nearest hazard, and the location of the next checkpoint. Look for walls or edges that can help guide the movement.
  </Step>
  <Step num="2" title="Choose the Caller">
    Decide which player will announce the route. The caller should use short instructions and avoid changing the plan unless a new hazard appears.
  </Step>
  <Step num="3" title="Apply a Small Burst">
    Start with a controlled movement input rather than maximum force. Short bursts make it easier to correct the shared character before reaching a spike or poison area.
  </Step>
  <Step num="4" title="Confirm the Position">
    Once the team reaches a safe ledge, wall, or checkpoint area, stop briefly. Check whether the character is facing the correct route before continuing.
  </Step>
  <Step num="5" title="Recover Deliberately">
    If the team becomes trapped or loses alignment, use the checkpoint or accept a reset. Repeating random inputs usually wastes more time than restarting from a known position.
  </Step>
</Steps>

| Obstacle Type | Safe Approach | Recovery Advice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spike section | Slow down and make small corrections | Reset if the route becomes misaligned |
| Poison hazard | Keep a clear line and avoid panic movement | Wait for a clean attempt |
| Wall bounce | Aim before applying the burst | Use the wall as a guide, not a target |
| Narrow passage | Use one caller and minimal input | Stop after each successful adjustment |
| Checkpoint approach | Prioritize stability over speed | Confirm the checkpoint before rushing onward |

Wall sections deserve special attention. A wall can help keep the team aligned, but pushing too strongly may launch the character past the safe route. When a wall is part of the solution, stay close to it and use gentle corrections. If the character begins moving backward, stop and restore the intended angle before applying more force.

Checkpoints change the best decision-making approach. Before reaching one, prioritize survival and alignment. After reaching one, experimentation becomes less costly because a failed attempt should not require repeating the entire course.

<Callout type="success" title="Checkpoint Strategy">
Treat every checkpoint as a new starting line. Pause, identify the next hazard, and agree on a fresh movement plan instead of carrying a rushed strategy forward.
</Callout>

<Section icon={Shield} title="Common Mistakes and Better Fixes">

Most failed attempts come from communication problems rather than a lack of speed. Players often push too hard, react too late, or continue making corrections after the character has already entered a dangerous position. Recognizing these patterns makes later sections more manageable.

| Common Mistake | Why It Fails | Better Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Holding movement too long | The character overshoots the safe route | Use brief bursts with a stop call |
| Both players steer at once | Inputs conflict and movement becomes unstable | Assign one active controller |
| Ignoring the route direction | The team may travel backward | Confirm the next landmark |
| Panicking near spikes | Fast corrections create extra collisions | Reduce input and use one direction |
| Repeating a failed attempt | The same mistake produces the same result | Change timing, angle, or caller |
| Rushing after a checkpoint | The team loses a stable position | Reassess before moving forward |

One frequent problem is assuming that more force will solve a difficult section. In practice, strong movement can make a section harder because the team has less time to react. Use maximum effort only when the route is open and the destination is obvious.

Another mistake is allowing frustration to replace planning. A failed attempt may reveal that the approach is too fast, the caller is unclear, or the team is entering from the wrong angle. Identify the specific problem before trying again.

<Section icon={Trophy} title="Beginner Progression and FAQ">

A good first session should focus on control rather than completion speed. Learn how the shared movement responds, practice short directional inputs, and use checkpoints to divide the course into manageable goals. Once communication becomes consistent, the same route should require fewer emergency corrections.

| Progress Goal | What to Practice | Completion Signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic control | Short directional bursts | The team can stop near a safe edge |
| Route reading | Identify hazards before moving | The caller gives warnings early |
| Checkpoint use | Pause after reaching progress points | The next section starts with a plan |
| Recovery | Reset without arguing or rushing | The team changes its approach |
| Advanced teamwork | Rotate caller and controller roles | Both players can lead a section |

The best co-op sessions are built around steady improvement. Reaching a new checkpoint is useful, but learning why a failed attempt happened is just as valuable. If a section repeatedly causes problems, simplify the plan: use one caller, reduce movement strength, and create a clear stop point.
